# Template Design Problem

This scenario originates from a colour‑printing company that manufactures a variety of items such as cartons and inserts
for human or animal food. Many products are offered in several marketable versions; the versions usually share the same
dimensions but differ by a small amount of printed text or colour. For instance, two cat‑food carton variations can be
identical in size yet differ only by having ‘Chicken Flavour’ on a blue background versus ‘Rab-bit Flavour’ on a green
backdrop.

An order typically asks for specific quantities of each version. Because every version fits the same board dimension, it
is known beforehand exactly how many units can be placed on one master sheet-the dimensions of this sheet are
essentially dictated by the printing press geometry. A master sheet is produced from a **template**, i.e., a thin metal
plate that bears etched outlines for several variations.

The core challenge therefore consists of two decisions:

1. Determine how many distinct templates will actually be prepared.
2. Allocate each variation and a chosen quantity of it to the slots of those templates, with the aim of using as few
   templates as needed while keeping waste material (un‑used board) to a minimum.

If a single template possessed more capacity than the total number of variations, theoretically a plan involving just
one template could meet the entire order. In practice this generates an uneconomical amount of scrap because it requires
printing many copies of every variation on each press run. By increasing the number of templates, we can drastically
lower overall waste, so the optimisation goal is to construct schemes for 1, 2,… templates that achieve the least
possible waste.

The following symbolic statements capture all requirements:

- Let **S** denote the capacity (number of slots) a single template can hold.
- Let **t** be the total number of templates selected for the design.
- Let **n** represent the count of distinct variations that must be produced.
- For each variation *i* (where *i* = 1 … **n**), let **d_i** record the required amount to be printed.
- Let **R_j** denote how many times template *j* will be pressed during production.
- Let **p_{i,j}** indicate how many slots of variation *i* are allocated to template *j*.

Constraints expressed are:

- The feasible total number of presses across all templates must stay between a theoretical minimum and twice that
  bound; we write this as "the sum of all **R_j** lies in the interval [lower, upper]", where *_lower_* is defined as
  `ceil(sum_i d_i / S)` and *upper* equals `2 * lower`.
- For every template, the sum of allocated slots across all variations must exactly equal its capacity **S**; i.e., for
  each j, `Σ_i p_{i,j} = S`.
- The production generated by the chosen allocations must be sufficient to satisfy every variation’s demand; formally,
  for each i, `Σ_j (p_{i,j} * R_j) ≥ d_i`.
- Variants that share identical demand are treated symmetrically: if two variations have equal **d** values, then the
  vector of slot allocations for those variants across all templates must be lexicographically non‑decreasing.
- An auxiliary symmetry condition ensures that when one variation’s demand is strictly less than the next variation’s
  demand, its total utilisation (sum of `p_{i,j} * R_j`) cannot exceed that of the later variant.

The optimisation objective is to minimise the aggregate number of presses across all selected templates; in other words,
we seek the smallest possible value of `Σ_j R_j`.
