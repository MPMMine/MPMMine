# Template Design Problem

This problem arises from a colour printing firm which produces a variety of products from thin board, including cartons for human and animal food and magazine inserts. Food products, for example, are often marketed as a basic brand with several variations (typically flavours). Packaging for such variations usually has the same overall design, in particular the same size and shape, but differs in a small proportion of the text displayed and/or in colour. For instance, two variations of a cat food carton may differ only in that on one is printed ‘Chicken Flavour’ on a blue background whereas the other has ‘Rabbit Flavour’ printed on a green background. A typical order is for a variety of quantities of several design variations. Because each variation is identical in dimension, we know in advance exactly how many items can be printed on each mother sheet of board, whose dimensions are largely determined by the dimensions of the printing machinery. Each mother sheet is printed from a template, consisting of a thin aluminium sheet on which the design for several of the variations is etched. The problem is to decide, firstly, how many distinct templates to produce, and secondly, which variations, and how many copies of each, to include on each template.


Each design of carton is made from an identically sized and shaped piece of board. `42` cartons can be printed on each mother sheet, and there are `30` different variations. We assume that there are `3` templates. The required number of printed variations are: `[280, 280, 230, 230, 230, 230, 150, 100, 100, 100, 100, 90, 90, 90, 90, 90, 90, 80, 80, 80, 80, 70, 70, 70, 70, 70, 70, 70, 60, 60]`

To improve solving efficiency, several symmetry-breaking and implied constraints are introduced. Variations with identical demand are symmetric; to break this symmetry, the rows of the allocation matrix corresponding to these variations must be lexicographically ordered. Furthermore, if a variation has a strictly smaller demand than another, its total printed production must not exceed that of the variation with the larger demand. The total surplus, defined as the total production across all templates multiplied by the number of slots minus the sum of all demands, serves to limit both individual and cumulative variation overproduction. Specifically, the surplus of any individual variation cannot exceed the total surplus, and the cumulative surplus of the first $k$ variations is also bounded by the total surplus. Additionally, the total production is bounded below by the minimum number of mother sheets needed to satisfy the total demand, and bounded above by twice that value. When precisely two templates are used, the number of pressings of the first template must be less than or equal to half of the total production, while the second must be greater than or equal to half. If exactly three templates are used, the pressings must satisfy ordered fractional bounds: the first template's pressings are at most one-third of the total production, the second template's pressings are at most half, and the third template's pressings are at least one-third.
